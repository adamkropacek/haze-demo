Animal welfare - the well-being of animals in livestock buildings

When refurbishing or designing a new livestock building for all age categories of cattle, it is important not to forget one essential criterion - the question of animal welfare. It also includes providing a suitable housing environment and protecting the animals against an adverse macroclimate - removing the physical and thermal factors of discomfort. High-quality ventilation systems in livestock buildings play a key role in this respect.

In cattle farming, the greatest risk to the welfare of the animals kept is housing them in a livestock building with inadequate air exchange - ventilation. Any shortcoming in the quality of the building environment, or housing environment, significantly increases the risk of disease (e.g. respiratory disease) and has a negative effect on a number of physiological processes in the animal's body that are closely linked to the productivity and reproduction of the animals kept.

We speak of thermal comfort when, within a certain temperature range and with the other physical parameters held constant, the thermal state of the organism is optimal and the animal expends only a negligible amount of energy on maintaining its physiological functions. This range of external temperatures is the so-called "thermoneutral zone". In cows the thermoneutral zone lies between -6 °C and + 19 °C, and in calves between +10 and +25 °C. Given the way temperatures have developed in recent years, in the summer months we are seeing the phenomenon of heat stress in cattle, particularly in high-yielding cows.

Cows enter heat stress when the ambient temperature rises above the level at which they can no longer dissipate metabolic heat from the body efficiently. Under heat stress, a number of thermoregulatory mechanisms are activated in cows to help them remove excess heat from the body. Outwardly, heat stress most often shows as an increased respiration rate and increased salivation; cows reduce their feed intake, drink more, seek out shaded places, spend less time lying down while spending more time standing (loading the limbs), and in some cases try to cool down by lying in wet/cold walking alleys.

In general, the heat stress threshold in cows is reached at roughly the point where the air temperature exceeds 21 °C. It is worth noting, however, that heat stress is usually a combination not only of ambient temperature but also of relative humidity. As both temperature and relative humidity rise, the risk of heat stress setting in increases very significantly. The immediate consequence of heat stress is usually not only a drop in milk production; changes in milk composition are also apparent, and farmers often face problems with cow reproduction as well (poorer conception rates, weaker signs of oestrus, early embryonic mortality and so on). The effects of high ambient temperatures - of heat stress - are long-lasting, persisting for up to several weeks.

With cold stress the situation is considerably simpler, precisely because it is easier for cows to "produce" metabolic heat in the rumen than to "get rid of" it. On the other hand, on ice days and arctic days livestock buildings suffer problems with water or slurry removal equipment freezing up; in buildings with minimal air exchange we can encounter high relative humidity and sometimes condensation as well.

Providing an adequate housing environment is the primary task of every farmer and needs to be considered as early as the construction stage, or when refurbishing an existing livestock building. Over the past few years the trend has been to build structurally simple livestock buildings whose design delivers an adequate housing environment. Effective natural air exchange in a livestock building is today most often achieved with open side walls fitted with roll-up curtains, combined with a good-quality ridge vent. Installing a weather station that automatically regulates the microclimate in the building also pays off for farmers.
